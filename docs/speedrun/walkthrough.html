The question I started with

Why do people who study hard plateau — and why does nothing warn them?

I assumed their tools measure memorization and sell it as readiness. That was half right and half badly wrong, and finding out which half took the whole project.

360s2:15

What the teardown found

I took apart six MCAT tools — UWorld, the Anki decks, Blueprint, Kaplan, Jack Westin, Memm.

The MCAT itself publishes a confidence band on every score: ±1 per section, ±2 total. The AAMC says the bands exist to discourage distinctions between applicants with similar scores.

Not one of the six tools publishes any interval at all. The products are more confident than the exam they predict.

From Blueprint's own published data:

7.2
point mean absolute error vs official scores
33
worst single miss — a 472 against an official 505
0 / 6
tools that reword a question on retest

472 is the literal floor of the scale. Reported to the student as a point estimate, no error bar.

The thing nobody measures

38–40%
of correct multiple-choice answers are guesses or low-confidence
.40 → .85
retention when those correct-but-guessed items get feedback

So the highest-value thing to review is the answer you got right and shouldn't have.

A percentage score cannot tell those apart. Ninety percent is ninety percent whether you knew it or guessed it.

The AAMC has collected per-question confidence data since October 2022. Four years. I could not find one student anywhere reporting they used it to change how they study.

The signal exists. Nothing is connected to it.

590s4:45

Where I was wrong

Longest beat. This is the strongest material — don't rush it.

I built a confident thesis: reviewing everything is why you're plateaued; volume of novel questions is what pays. Then I ran three things against it — a steelman told to destroy it, a replication audit, and a check against the field's own consensus documents.

Four of my citations died.

What I claimedWhat the evidence says
Review doesn't matter; volume doesI had the paper inverted. With feedback g = 0.73, without 0.39. Review is what pays. Volume was not significant.
Nothing on the market measures transferFlatly false. AAMC full-lengths, UWorld and Blueprint all score novel passages.
Show students the gap and they'll reallocateMy own citation predicts they won't — told 90% of people learn better a given way, 78% put themselves in the 10%.
My best finding, "and it's an RCT"Not an RCT. Zero replications across 159 citations in ten years.

That last one had one more thing wrong with it. Its senior author holds book royalties and co-founded a company selling the curriculum it supports — disclosed nowhere.

It was the only commercial conflict in an eight-paper stack, and it was sitting under my favourite finding.

Two instructions caused all of this, and neither was mine: don't search for evidence that confirms your predictions, and record who funded each study.

645s5:30

So what survives

Almost nothing that's an effect-size claim — because in education, effect sizes don't survive scale.

0.13%
of education findings are ever replicated
1 of 7
programmes surviving scale-up; mean 0.25 → 0.01

So the points of view deliberately don't rest on any effect surviving replication. They rest on what instruments are blind to:

  • A percentage can't tell a guess from knowledge — and that's a third of your correct answers.
  • "Evidence-based" is a claim nobody in this market can support, including us.
  • Retention optimisation was never a readiness claim. FSRS minimises study-minutes-per-recall-probability. There is no exam in that objective function.

Third point is worth a beat of silence. The schedulers are honest; the inference downstream isn't.

745s6:15

What that forced us to build

Each point of view had to change the code, or it wasn't a point of view.

  • The engine refuses to emit a score below an evidence threshold — a refusal, not a number with wide error bars. A caller that never receives a number can't render one.
  • On iOS a score is an enum, so rendering a refusal as a number is a compile error.
  • Coverage is checked against the official AAMC outline, all 31 content categories — so what you haven't studied is visible. Right now: 1 of 31.
  • Readiness reports on the real 472–528 scale with its range, coverage, and reasons — or is withheld.

The guarantee is structural, not a promise in a README.

830s6:45

Close on falsifiability

Every point of view states what would kill it. The headline one:

If confident-and-wrong rate predicts outcomes no better than raw accuracy, the whole thesis is dead.

And two objections I have not answered, which are in the document rather than buried:

  • The memory-minus-performance gap is a difference score — reliability around .50.
  • Our confidence intervals are too narrow: they model sampling error when the dominant variance is passage-to-passage.

A tool whose whole pitch is honest measurement doesn't get to hide its own weakest numbers.

If asked

Isn't this just spaced repetition with extra steps?
No. Spaced repetition optimises time-per-unit-recall. We measure whether recall survives a change in wording — a different quantity — and refuse to report it when we can't.
How do you know the gap is real?
We don't yet. That's the ablation test, and it hasn't run. The claim on the record is the measurement one — that percent-correct is blind to guessing — which is verifiable independently of whether the gap predicts scores.
Why should I trust your numbers over UWorld's?
You shouldn't trust them more. You should notice ours come with a range and a refusal state, and theirs don't.
What's the weakest part?
The intervals are too narrow, and there's no student outcome data behind the score mapping — it's a documented linear placeholder, not a fitted model. Both are stated in the document.
