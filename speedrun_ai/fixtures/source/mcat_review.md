## Glycolysis

Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol and proceeds without oxygen, splitting one glucose into two molecules of pyruvate. The pathway invests two ATP in its early steps and recovers four, for a net yield of two ATP and two NADH per glucose. Phosphofructokinase-1 catalyses the committed, rate-limiting step, converting fructose-6-phosphate into fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. That enzyme is inhibited by ATP and by citrate, and it is activated by AMP and by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate.

Hexokinase traps glucose inside the cell by phosphorylating it, and in the liver the higher-Km isoform glucokinase performs the same job only when glucose is abundant. Under anaerobic conditions pyruvate is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, which regenerates the NAD+ that glycolysis needs to keep running. Red blood cells depend on this route entirely, since they have no mitochondria at all. The lactate released can be carried to the liver and rebuilt into glucose through gluconeogenesis, an exchange known as the Cori cycle.

## Cellular respiration

The citric acid cycle runs in the mitochondrial matrix, where each acetyl-CoA that enters releases two molecules of carbon dioxide. One turn of the cycle produces three NADH, one FADH2 and one GTP, so one glucose yields double that because it supplies two acetyl-CoA. Isocitrate dehydrogenase is the cycle's rate-limiting enzyme and is stimulated by ADP and inhibited by ATP and NADH. Pyruvate dehydrogenase links glycolysis to the cycle and is inhibited by its own products, acetyl-CoA and NADH.

Electrons from NADH and FADH2 pass along the inner mitochondrial membrane to complexes that pump protons into the intermembrane space. The resulting electrochemical gradient drives protons back through ATP synthase, which phosphorylates ADP in a process called chemiosmotic coupling. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor at complex IV, and blocking it stops the whole chain and the gradient with it. An uncoupler such as 2,4-dinitrophenol carries protons across the membrane directly, so respiration accelerates while ATP production falls and the energy leaves as heat.

## Enzyme kinetics

The Michaelis constant Km is the substrate concentration at which an enzyme runs at half of its maximum velocity, and it is inversely related to the enzyme's apparent affinity. Vmax is proportional to the total enzyme concentration, so adding more enzyme raises Vmax but leaves Km unchanged. A competitive inhibitor binds the active site, raising the apparent Km while leaving Vmax untouched, because a high substrate concentration can outcompete it. A pure noncompetitive inhibitor binds an allosteric site and lowers Vmax without changing Km.

Enzymes accelerate reactions by lowering the activation energy, and they never change the equilibrium constant or the free energy difference between reactants and products. Cooperative enzymes with several subunits give sigmoidal rather than hyperbolic velocity curves, so their behaviour is described by the Hill equation instead. Covalent modification such as phosphorylation and proteolytic cleavage of a zymogen are both irreversible-style controls compared with rapid allosteric regulation. Optimal activity depends on pH and temperature, and denaturation past those optima destroys tertiary structure without breaking peptide bonds.

## Proteins

The twenty standard amino acids share an alpha carbon bearing an amino group, a carboxyl group and a distinguishing side chain, and all except glycine are chiral. Side-chain pKa values determine charge at a given pH: aspartate and glutamate are negative at physiological pH, lysine and arginine are positive, and histidine sits near neutrality with a pKa around six. The isoelectric point is the pH at which the molecule carries no net charge, and a protein does not migrate in an electric field there. Peptide bonds have partial double-bond character, which keeps the backbone amide planar and restricts rotation.

Secondary structure comprises alpha helices and beta sheets held together by backbone hydrogen bonds, while tertiary structure is driven largely by the hydrophobic effect burying nonpolar side chains. Disulfide bridges between cysteine residues covalently lock tertiary structure and are the only covalent bond among the higher-order interactions. Quaternary structure describes the assembly of separate polypeptide subunits, as in the four chains of haemoglobin. Denaturing agents such as urea and heat unfold proteins by disrupting these noncovalent interactions.

## Membranes

Biological membranes are fluid bilayers of amphipathic phospholipids, with hydrophilic heads facing water and hydrophobic tails facing inward. Cholesterol buffers fluidity, keeping the membrane from becoming too rigid when cold and too permeable when warm. Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse straight through, whereas ions and large polar molecules require protein channels or carriers. Simple diffusion needs no protein and no energy, and it always runs down a concentration gradient.

Facilitated diffusion moves solute down its gradient through a protein and saturates once every transporter is occupied. Primary active transport hydrolyses ATP directly, as the sodium-potassium pump does when it exports three sodium ions for every two potassium ions imported. Secondary active transport spends no ATP itself and instead rides the gradient that primary transport established, which is how the sodium-glucose cotransporter works. Osmosis is the diffusion of water toward the more concentrated solution, and tonicity describes the direction water will move across a given membrane.

## Acids and bases

A Bronsted-Lowry acid donates a proton and its conjugate base accepts one, and the stronger the acid the weaker its conjugate base. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation states that pH equals pKa plus the logarithm of the ratio of conjugate base to acid. A buffer resists pH change and works best within about one pH unit of its pKa, where that ratio is close to one. At exactly one half of the equivalence volume in a titration of a weak acid, the pH equals the pKa.

At the equivalence point of a weak acid titrated with strong base, the solution contains only the conjugate base, so the pH is above seven. Titrating a strong acid with a strong base instead gives an equivalence point at pH seven, since neither product ion hydrolyses. Blood is buffered mainly by the bicarbonate system, in which carbon dioxide, water, carbonic acid and bicarbonate sit in equilibrium. Hyperventilation blows off carbon dioxide and pulls that equilibrium toward the left, raising blood pH and producing respiratory alkalosis.

## Substitution reactions

The bimolecular substitution pathway proceeds in one concerted step, so its rate depends on both the substrate and the nucleophile. It requires backside attack, which inverts the configuration at the carbon under attack and is blocked by bulky groups, so methyl and primary substrates react fastest. Polar aprotic solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide accelerate it because they leave the nucleophile relatively unencumbered. Strong, negatively charged nucleophiles favour this route over the alternatives.

The unimolecular pathway forms a carbocation in a slow first step, so its rate depends only on the substrate concentration. Tertiary substrates react fastest here, because alkyl groups stabilise the carbocation by induction and hyperconjugation, and the planar intermediate leads to a racemic mixture of products. Polar protic solvents help by stabilising both the leaving group and the cation through hydrogen bonding. Elimination competes with both routes, and heat and bulky strong bases push the outcome toward the alkene.

## Spectroscopy

In proton nuclear magnetic resonance, the chemical shift reports the electronic environment of a hydrogen and is measured in parts per million from tetramethylsilane. Electronegative neighbours deshield nearby hydrogens and move their signals downfield, which is why an aldehyde hydrogen appears near nine to ten parts per million. The area under a peak is proportional to the number of hydrogens in that environment, and splitting into n plus one lines reveals how many hydrogens sit on adjacent carbons. A carboxylic acid hydrogen is the most downfield common signal, often past ten parts per million and usually broad.

Infrared spectroscopy identifies functional groups by bond vibration, with a carbonyl stretch near seventeen hundred wavenumbers and a broad alcohol stretch above three thousand. Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy probes conjugated pi systems, and greater conjugation shifts absorption to longer wavelengths. Mass spectrometry reports the mass-to-charge ratio, so the molecular ion peak gives the molecular weight of the intact compound. Chromatographic separations sort compounds by their differing affinity for a stationary phase against a mobile phase.

## Fluids and circuits

For an incompressible fluid the volume flow rate is constant, so a narrower cross-section forces a higher flow speed. Bernoulli's equation conserves energy per unit volume along a streamline, which means the faster region of an ideal flow is also the lower-pressure region. Hydrostatic pressure increases with depth as the density times gravitational acceleration times depth, and it does not depend on the shape of the container. A submerged object experiences a buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces.

In a series circuit the same current passes through every element and the resistances add directly. In a parallel arrangement each branch sees the same voltage, the reciprocals of the resistances add, and the equivalent resistance is smaller than any single branch. Adding a parallel branch therefore lowers total resistance and increases the current drawn from the source. Power dissipated in a resistor equals the current squared times the resistance, and an ideal battery maintains its terminal voltage regardless of the load.

## Learning and stress

Classical conditioning pairs a neutral stimulus with one that already produces a response until the neutral stimulus produces it alone. In operant conditioning consequences change the frequency of a behaviour, and reinforcement always increases that frequency while punishment always decreases it. Negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus in order to strengthen a response, which is why it is not a form of punishment. Variable ratio schedules reinforce after an unpredictable number of responses and are the most resistant to extinction.

Encoding, storage and retrieval are the three stages of memory, and retrieval is easier when the cues present at encoding are present again. Recognition supplies those cues directly and is therefore easier than free recall, which requires generating the material unaided. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis responds to stressors by releasing corticotropin-releasing hormone, then adrenocorticotropic hormone, then cortisol from the adrenal cortex. Chronically elevated cortisol raises blood glucose, suppresses immune function, and feeds back negatively on the hypothalamus and pituitary.
